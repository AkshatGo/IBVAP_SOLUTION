The IBVAP Manual

Everything the system does, why it does it that way, what every term means, and where the honest limits are. Written to be read start to finish.

01 What IBVAP is

Start here. One paragraph, no jargon.

In plain terms

IBVAP watches a camera feed and does four things a human guard does, without getting tired: it spots people and vehicles, it notices when someone crosses a line they shouldn't, it reads number plates, and it notices when someone tampers with the camera itself. Every alert it raises is written into a log that cannot be secretly edited — not even by us.

It's built to run on the camera site itself, not in a data centre. That constraint shapes every decision in the system: the models are small, the pipeline degrades rather than crashes when hardware is weak, and nothing depends on a reliable network link.

Concretely, it is a Python system with three runnable entry points — a Streamlit dashboard, a FastAPI server with 16 endpoints and a live WebSocket, and a command-line demo — sitting on top of seven edge modules and two fine-tuned neural networks, covered by 159 automated tests.

02 Why it exists

The problem is not "we don't have cameras". It's that cameras alone don't work.

Border and perimeter sites already have cameras. What they lack is anyone reliably watching them. Three specific failures follow, and each maps to a feature we built:

Failure 1 — Nobody is watching at 3am

A human monitoring a wall of feeds loses effective attention within about twenty minutes. Intrusions are typically discovered on review, after the fact — which converts a prevention problem into a forensics problem. Our answer: automated detection, tracking, and virtual fence alerts that fire the moment a boundary is crossed.

Failure 2 — A disabled camera looks like a quiet camera

This is the failure people underestimate. If someone covers, blinds, or unplugs a camera, the feed goes dark or still — which on a monitoring wall looks exactly like "nothing is happening". The most dangerous moment produces the least alarming display. Our answer: treat the absence of signal as an event in itself, at critical severity.

Failure 3 — Off-the-shelf models don't understand Indian roads

Every general-purpose detection model is trained on COCO, a Western-collected dataset. It has no auto-rickshaw class. It has never seen Indian traffic density. Deploying it unchanged means systematically misreading the environment. Our answer: fine-tune on Indian data, and measure the difference.

Failure 4 — Evidence that can be quietly edited isn't evidence

A detection log stored in a normal database can be altered by anyone with access, including an insider. If the log is the basis for an accusation, its integrity has to be demonstrable, not assumed. Our answer: a cryptographic hash chain where any edit is detectable and localisable.

03 The X-factor

What we offer that a competent team using off-the-shelf parts would not.

Be precise about this, because "we use AI for surveillance" is not a differentiator — everyone says it. Four things actually are.

X-factor 1 — Detection that can name what it sees

This is the strongest one, and it is structural rather than incremental. COCO — the dataset behind every off-the-shelf detector — has no auto-rickshaw class. An off-the-shelf model cannot be right about an auto-rickshaw; it can only pick the least-wrong category it does have.

Auto-rickshaw is our best-performing class at 0.617 mAP@0.5, better than car or person. That is the entire domain-adaptation argument in one number.

X-factor 2 — The camera itself is a sensor

Most systems analyse what the camera shows. We also analyse what the camera is doing. Four tamper heuristics run on every frame: sustained black frames (covered lens), white-out (sensor failure or floodlight attack), a sudden brightness collapse (gradual interference), and timeout (link cut). Each fires a critical alert into the audit chain.

X-factor 3 — Evidence integrity, demonstrable live

Every alert is hash-chained to its predecessor. Editing any record breaks the chain from that point forward, and the verifier reports the exact index. You can demonstrate this in thirty seconds on camera: corrupt a record deliberately, run verify, watch it get caught. Very few teams can show a falsifiable integrity guarantee.

X-factor 4 — It degrades instead of failing

Three independent fallback chains, all tested:

  • Detection: fine-tuned YOLO → stock YOLO → HOG → motion detection
  • Plate finding: trained localiser → classical contour detection (no model, no GPU)
  • Tracking: no optional dependencies at all — it always works identically

This is what makes a Tier-2 deployment on cheap hardware real rather than aspirational. Same pipeline, same code, no GPU.

04 Concepts you need

Every term a judge might ask you to define. Learn these — being unable to explain your own metric is the fastest way to lose a room.

Object detection (vs. classification)

Classification answers "what is in this image?" — one label for the whole picture. Detection answers "what is in this image, and where?" — it returns a list of boxes, each with a class label and a confidence score. IBVAP needs detection because position is the whole point: an object's location is what determines whether it crossed a fence.

Bounding box and confidence

A box is four numbers — x1, y1, x2, y2 — the corners of a rectangle in pixels. Confidence is the model's self-reported certainty, 0 to 1. It is not a probability of being correct; it's a calibrated-ish score you threshold on.

IoU — Intersection over Union

How much two boxes overlap: the area they share divided by the area they jointly cover. 1.0 means identical, 0 means no overlap. It's how you decide whether a predicted box "counts as" a real one. The convention IoU ≥ 0.5 means the boxes overlap by at least half.

Precision, recall, F1

MetricQuestion it answersFailure it punishes
PrecisionOf what I flagged, how much was real?False alarms
RecallOf what was real, how much did I catch?Misses
F1Balanced single scoreBeing lopsided

Which matters more depends on the deployment, and this is a real decision you made: at a border, a missed intruder costs more than a false alert an operator dismisses. So we favour recall — which is why we lowered the confidence threshold from 0.45 to 0.25 after measuring.

mAP — mean Average Precision

The standard detection score. For each class you sweep every possible threshold, plot precision against recall, and take the area under that curve (Average Precision). Then average across classes — hence mean.

  • mAP@0.5 — boxes count as correct at IoU ≥ 0.5. The headline number.
  • mAP@0.5:0.95 — averaged over IoU thresholds 0.5 to 0.95. Much stricter; it punishes loose boxes. Ours is lower (0.336 vs 0.508), which tells you the model finds objects more reliably than it outlines them tightly.

Transfer learning and fine-tuning

Training a detector from scratch needs millions of images. Instead you take a model already trained on a huge general dataset — it has learned edges, textures, shapes — and continue training it on your smaller specific dataset. It keeps the general visual knowledge and adapts the specifics. That is what we did: both our models started from COCO-pretrained YOLOv8n.

Freezing the backbone

A detector splits roughly into a backbone (learns general visual features) and a head (turns features into class predictions). On a small dataset, letting everything change immediately can destroy the good general features — "catastrophic forgetting". So we froze the backbone for the first 10 epochs, training only the head, then unfroze everything for 40 more. Warm up, then adapt.

Epoch, batch, learning rate

An epoch is one full pass over the training set. A batch is how many images the GPU processes at once — bigger is faster but needs more VRAM (we used 8 because your card has 4GB). The learning rate is how big a step the model takes when correcting itself.

Overfitting and held-out validation

A model can memorise its training data instead of learning generalisable patterns — scoring brilliantly on what it has seen and failing on anything new. The defence is to hold back a portion of data the model never trains on, and only report scores on that. Every number in this project is on held-out data. If someone challenges your results, this is your answer.

Multi-object tracking and track IDs

Detection is per-frame and stateless — it doesn't know the car in frame 2 is the same car from frame 1. Tracking assigns a persistent track ID so an object can be followed over time. Our tracker matches boxes between frames by IoU, keeps a track alive briefly when a detection is missed, and never matches across classes.

Why this matters more than it sounds

Three separate features depend on stable track IDs: fence entry/exit (you must know the object was outside before it was inside), speed and bearing (needs a trajectory), and ANPR consensus (votes are grouped per vehicle). We found a bug where the tracker assigned a new ID every frame — all three broke silently, with nothing crashing.

Point-in-polygon (the virtual fence)

A fence zone is a polygon of pixel coordinates. Each frame, the system asks whether an object's centre point lies inside it. The alert fires on the transition from outside to inside — not on presence, or a stationary object would alarm forever.

OCR and multi-frame consensus

OCR reads text from an image. On a moving vehicle it is noisy: the same plate might read MH12AB1234, MH12A81234, NH12AB1234 across three frames. Consensus voting collects readings per track ID and takes the majority. Our API returns the individual readings and the tally, so an operator can see why a plate was accepted rather than trusting a bare string.

Hash chain

A cryptographic hash (SHA-256) turns any input into a fixed-length fingerprint; changing one character changes the fingerprint completely. In a chain, each record contains the hash of the record before it. Edit record 5 and record 6's stored "previous hash" no longer matches — and so does every record after. One tampered entry invalidates the entire tail, and the verifier reports exactly where.

Edge computing

Running computation at the site rather than sending video to a central server. Why it matters here: bandwidth at remote posts is scarce, latency matters for alerts, and a system that stops working when the link drops is not a security system.

ONNX

A portable model format. You train in PyTorch, export to ONNX, and run it in a different runtime — including ones optimised for specific hardware like NVIDIA's TensorRT. Exporting is the prerequisite for hardware optimisation, not the optimisation itself. Be precise about that distinction.

05 Architecture

Seven modules, one pipeline, three entry points.

src/edge/                 # everything that runs at the camera site
  detector.py            # finds objects in a frame
  tracker.py             # gives them persistent IDs across frames
  fence.py               # decides if a tracked object crossed a zone
  anpr.py                # finds and reads number plates
  signal.py              # watches the camera for tampering
  hashchain.py           # tamper-evident event log
  pipeline.py            # orchestrates all six, per frame

src/backend/api.py       # 16 REST endpoints + live WebSocket
src/config.py            # every tunable in one place, env-overridable

scripts/                 # data → training → evaluation → export
tests/                   # 159 tests, ~4s, no downloads
models/weights/          # the two trained models (versioned)

The design rule worth internalising: each module does one thing and knows nothing about the others. The fence doesn't know what a neural network is — it receives a track ID and a point. The hash chain doesn't know what an intrusion is — it receives a dictionary. Only pipeline.py knows the full sequence.

This is why the test suite can run in four seconds with no model downloads: the pipeline is tested with stubbed detections, because the contract between modules is what matters, not the neural network behind it.

06 What happens to a single frame

The whole system, in the order it executes. This is the walkthrough to have ready.

1
Signal check — before anything else
The frame is checked for tampering first: is it black, white-out, a sudden brightness collapse, or absent entirely? A blinded camera is an event, so this runs before detection rather than after. Fires critical severity.
2
Detection
YOLOv8n runs on the frame and returns boxes with class labels and confidence, filtered to the classes we act on. Class names are read from the model itself — the fine-tuned model has seven classes, stock COCO has eighty.
3
Tracking
Each detection is matched by IoU to an existing track, or starts a new one. Unmatched tracks age out after a buffer. Output: the same detections, now with persistent track IDs and accumulated trajectories.
4
Fence evaluation
For each tracked object, is its centre inside any zone? If it wasn't before and is now, that's an intrusion — logged with the zone's severity, the object's speed and bearing, and a human-readable explanation string.
5
ANPR on vehicles
Vehicle detections are cropped and passed to the plate localiser (trained model, or contour fallback). Each plate region goes to OCR, and readings accumulate per track ID until a majority emerges.
6
Everything notable becomes a chain event
Signal loss, fence intrusion, and confident plate matches each become a record: hashed, linked to the previous record, appended to disk. Callbacks fire for any downstream consumer.
7
Annotation and return
Boxes, zones, trajectories and a status HUD are drawn on a copy of the frame — never the original. The caller gets a structured result: detections, tracks, intrusions, plates, alerts, camera status, and the current chain head hash.
The ordering is a design decision

Signal check before detection, and fence evaluation after tracking, are not arbitrary. You cannot detect anything in a blinded frame, and you cannot know something "crossed" a line without knowing where it was previously. If asked why the pipeline is ordered this way, that's the answer.

Model 2 — the plate localiser

In plain terms

Give it a photo of a vehicle; it draws a box around the number plate. It does not read the plate — that's OCR's job. It only finds where the plate is, so the OCR gets a tight crop instead of a whole car.

This one has the strongest result in the project, because it replaced something measurably bad. The original approach was classical computer vision: find edges, find four-sided contours, keep the ones with a plate-like aspect ratio. It worked poorly.

LocaliserPrecisionRecallF1False positives
Contour (before)0.2500.1580.19448
Trained (after)0.9290.9010.9157

The contour method found roughly one plate in six and produced three false positives for every true one. The trained model finds 91 of 101 with 7 false positives. F1 improved 4.7×.

Why this is your best result

Not because 0.915 is high, but because it is attributable. You identified your weakest component, replaced it with a trained model, and measured both on the same held-out images. That's a methodology story. "Our mAP is 0.9" is a leaderboard claim anyone can make; "we improved our weakest component 4.7× and here's the before" is engineering.

What we deliberately did not train

OCR. We use EasyOCR unmodified. Fine-tuning a text-recognition model is a much larger undertaking with poor payoff compared to fixing localisation — which was the actual bottleneck. Give OCR a tight, correct crop and it performs; give it a whole car and it fails regardless of how good it is.

The honest consequence: end-to-end plate accuracy is unmeasured, because we have no ground-truth plate strings. Never quote an ANPR accuracy number.

08 Why we chose what we chose

Every one of these is a question you may be asked. The reasoning matters more than the choice.

Why YOLOv8-nano and not something bigger

The deployment target decides model size, not the accuracy leaderboard. A border post runs constrained hardware, so 5.9MB and CPU-viable inference isn't a compromise — it's the requirement. Moving to yolov8s/m is a config change if Tier-1 hardware budget allows, but it would break the Tier-2 story.

Why we kept the classical plate detector after training a better one

Because it needs no model and no GPU. It becomes the Tier-2 fallback path: a site with cheap hardware runs the same pipeline with degraded but functional ANPR. Deleting the inferior method would have made the system less deployable.

Why we lowered the confidence threshold from 0.45 to 0.25

Because we measured it rather than assuming. A sweep showed F1 peaks at 0.25 for the fine-tuned model; 0.45 bought precision at the cost of 5.5 points of recall. At a border, a missed intruder costs more than a false alert an operator dismisses. The original 0.45 was chosen against stock COCO weights and did not survive fine-tuning — which is exactly why you re-validate thresholds after changing a model.

Why we wrote our own tracker

Not by preference — by necessity. The original code tried to import ByteTrack from two different packages and silently fell back to assigning a new ID every frame when both failed. That's not a tracker; it's a no-op that breaks three downstream features. A simple greedy-IoU tracker with no optional dependencies behaves identically everywhere, which for edge deployment matters more than sophistication.

Why 640×640 input

The standard YOLO training size, and the sweet spot between small-object recall and compute. Dropping to 416 would speed inference meaningfully if a Jetson demands it, at the cost of missing distant figures — which is the wrong trade for perimeter security.

Why the hash chain is a JSONL file, not a database

Because an append-only text file has no server, no schema migration, and no configuration to get wrong at a remote site. It survives restart, is trivially auditable, and can be copied off with scp. PostgreSQL is on the roadmap for aggregation, but the local integrity guarantee shouldn't depend on a running service.

Why we kept auto-rickshaw as a seventh class instead of mapping it to "car"

Because it's the whole point. Folding it into an existing class would have made the dataset simpler and erased the one result that demonstrates domain adaptation.

09 The data

What we trained on, how it was prepared, and where it is thin.

How raw data becomes trainable

Datasets ship in incompatible formats — PASCAL-VOC XML, per-image text files, custom JSON. Everything converges on one YOLO format: one .txt per image, one line per object, class cx cy w h normalised to 0–1. Normalising to relative coordinates is what makes labels survive resizing.

Two preparation steps worth understanding:

  • Class remapping. IDD has 15 classes; we act on 7. rider folds into person; vehicle fallback, caravan and trailer fold into truck; animal, traffic light, traffic sign and train are dropped.
  • Deduplication. IDD is dashcam footage at 30fps, so consecutive frames are near-identical. We hash each frame and drop ones too similar to the last kept frame — compared against the last kept rather than the previous one, so a slow pan can't ratchet through in sub-threshold steps.
Correction you must know

The DataCluster "Indian Number Plates" dataset is widely described as 10,000 images. What Kaggle hosts is a 47-image free sample of a commercial product. We discovered this by downloading it. If you cite dataset sizes, cite what you actually have — 460 plate images total, not 10,000. Being caught inflating a dataset size would undermine every other number you present.

Where the data is thin

  • Bicycle — 614 instances. The weakest class, directly because of this.
  • Night — no low-light training data at all. ExDark is planned, not used.
  • Plate strings — no ground-truth text, so end-to-end OCR is unmeasurable.
  • Actual border footage — IDD is urban road scenes. We have no border-specific imagery, and collecting it is a multi-week programme in itself.

10 What you must know as the creator

The things that will hurt if you don't know them, and the things you should be proud of.

The six bugs — and why they matter more than they sound

Every one was silent: the code ran, produced plausible output, and did the wrong thing. This is the failure mode that survives casual testing and dies in a demo.

BugWhat it silently broke

The lesson to carry: a fix without a test isn't finished. Each of these now has a regression test, and each test was verified by confirming it fails against the old code. A test that passes against broken code guards nothing.

The claim discipline

Never say these

"Running on Jetson" · "TensorRT-accelerated" · "edge-optimised" · any on-device FPS · any end-to-end ANPR accuracy · "face recognition" · "10,000 plate images" · any real-time claim you haven't measured.

Always safe

"Edge-class YOLOv8-nano, 3M parameters, 5.9MB, 22 FPS on CPU alone" · "F1 improved from 0.194 to 0.915 against the baseline we replaced" · "auto-rickshaw is our best class at 0.617, and COCO has no such class" · "159 automated tests" · "ONNX exported and verified through onnxruntime; TensorRT is roadmap".

The reason for the discipline is not modesty. It's that one unbacked claim contaminates every backed one. If a judge catches you overstating hardware deployment, they will start doubting your mAP too — and your mAP is real.

What you should genuinely be proud of

  • Two models fine-tuned on real data, both measured against the baselines they replaced
  • A structural differentiator, not a marginal one — the auto-rickshaw result is not a tuning win, it's a capability that didn't exist
  • 159 tests on a hackathon project, with regression tests verified to actually bite
  • Documentation that states what isn't built — rarer and more credible than documentation that oversells
  • An integrity guarantee you can demonstrate live and let someone try to break

Edge deployment — making the Tier-1 story real

  • TensorRT conversion — take the verified ONNX and build a hardware engine. Concept: kernel fusion and precision calibration — the runtime merges operations and rewrites them for the specific GPU.
  • INT8 quantisation — store weights as 8-bit integers instead of 32-bit floats. Roughly 4× smaller and substantially faster, at a small accuracy cost. Needs a calibration dataset to choose the scaling.
  • Jetson benchmarking — the actual missing evidence. Requires an Orin Nano and an ARM64 Docker image; the current one is x86-only.

Models worth considering later

  • YOLOv8s/m — bigger backbone, better accuracy, only if Tier-1 hardware budget allows. Don't do it reflexively.
  • A thermal-imagery detector — the genuine answer to night surveillance, and a different sensor rather than a better model. Would need a thermal dataset and a second camera.
  • Fine-tuned OCR (CRNN or PaddleOCR) — only after localisation is saturated and you have plate-string ground truth. Do not start here.
  • A tracker with re-identification embeddings — replaces IoU matching with appearance features, which is what makes tracking survive occlusion and enables cross-camera correlation.
Sequencing advice

Resist doing the interesting thing before the useful thing. Cross-camera re-identification is more exciting than fixing the bicycle class, but the bicycle class is a number on your own slide that a judge can point at. Close your measured weaknesses before opening new fronts.

12 The full pitch

Roughly three minutes spoken. Learn the shape, not the words.

Open — the problem, 30 seconds

"Border sites already have cameras. What they don't have is anyone reliably watching them. A human monitoring a wall of feeds loses effective attention in about twenty minutes — so intrusions get discovered on review, after the fact. And there's a worse failure: if someone covers or cuts a camera, the feed goes still. On a monitoring wall, a disabled camera looks exactly like a quiet one. The most dangerous moment produces the least alarming display."

The gap — 30 seconds

"You could point an off-the-shelf detector at this, and teams do. But every general-purpose model is trained on COCO, a Western dataset. It has no auto-rickshaw class. It has never seen Indian traffic density. So it doesn't just perform worse — it systematically misreads the environment it's deployed in."

The proof — 40 seconds ⭐

"Here's the same frame through both models. On the left, stock YOLOv8: it calls one auto-rickshaw a truck, and the other one a couch. On the right, ours, fine-tuned on the Indian Driving Dataset: both are auto-rickshaws, 94% confidence. We didn't make the label more accurate — we made it possible. Auto-rickshaw is now our best class, 0.617 mAP, better than car or person."

What we built — 40 seconds

"IBVAP is a full edge pipeline: detection, tracking, a multi-zone virtual fence, number plate recognition, and camera tamper detection. Three things make it more than a wrapper around a model.

"First — the camera itself is a sensor. Black frames, white-out, a sudden brightness collapse, or a dead link all raise critical alerts. A blinded camera is an event.

"Second — every alert is hash-chained. Edit any record and verification fails and names the exact record. I can break it live and show you it getting caught.

"Third — it degrades instead of failing. No GPU? It falls back to classical computer vision, same pipeline, no code change. The models are 5.9MB and run at 22 frames a second on a laptop CPU with no accelerator at all."

The engineering — 25 seconds

"We also fixed our weakest component with data. Plate localisation was classical contour detection — it found one plate in six. We trained a localiser and measured both on the same held-out images: F1 went from 0.19 to 0.92. A 4.7× improvement, measured against the thing it replaced. The whole system has 159 automated tests, and every regression test was verified by checking it fails against the old code."

Close — the honesty, 25 seconds

"I'll tell you what we haven't done, because it matters. We have not run this on a Jetson — no device has executed this code. We've exported to ONNX and verified it, but TensorRT and hardware benchmarking are next. Our datasets are modest: 8,700 detection images, 460 plate images. Our weakest class is bicycle, at 0.309, and that's because we only had 614 training instances of it.

"What we do have is a working pipeline, two models fine-tuned on Indian data, both measured against the baselines they replaced, and documentation that says exactly what isn't built. Everything I've quoted is reproducible from the repository with one command."

Why close on the gaps

Counter-intuitive, but it's the strongest move available. Volunteering your limits makes every other number credible — you've demonstrated you distinguish measured from assumed. A judge who has to extract your weaknesses trusts your strengths less than one you handed them to.
